How to Speed Up Your Website by Optimizing Images

Images are the largest asset on most web pages, and the fastest way to improve load time is to optimise them before you upload. This guide covers format selection, compression, lazy loading, and how to measure the improvement on your own site.

Why Images Are the Bottleneck for Page Speed

A typical homepage sends around 60 percent of its total data weight in images, and that figure has grown year over year as pages add hero photographs, embedded graphics, and video thumbnails. Every extra kilobyte that has to travel from the server to the visitor's screen increases the time until the first pixel renders and the time until the page becomes fully usable. Slow pages also rank worse in search results, and they produce more bounce.

The encouraging news is that image optimisation is one of the highest-return fixes you can make. Because images make up the largest share of page weight, a modest reduction in file size translates directly into a visible speed improvement. Unlike a redesign or a server upgrade, the change is cheap and can be measured in minutes.

The Four Levers of Image Optimisation

  1. Choose the right format. Use JPEG for photographs, PNG for graphics with text and transparency, and WebP where the browser supports it. WebP often delivers the same quality at a significantly smaller file size.
  2. Compress the image. Lossy compression can reduce a file's size by 40–60% while remaining invisible to the eye, provided you choose a sensible quality setting and preview the result.
  3. Resize to the display dimensions. Delivering a 4000-pixel-wide image to be displayed at 800 pixels is a waste of data. Set the width and height attributes to match the rendered size.
  4. Serve next-generation formats. Modern browsers can use WebP and AVIF. Providing a single WebP variant with a JPEG fallback covers the widest audience without a substantial size penalty.

Format Selection: A Teaching Example

Consider a screenshot of a mobile app interface. It contains sharp text, rounded corners, flat colour blocks, and a semi-transparent status bar. Save it as JPEG, and the edges of the text will fray; the file will be larger than you expect, and the transparency will be filled with a solid white rectangle. Save it as PNG, and the file will be small enough for a document but no smaller. Convert it to WebP, and the same image becomes a fraction of the size while retaining all the sharpness and transparency. Compressing Without Losing What Matters

Lossy compression works by discarding information the human eye is least likely to notice. The art is in finding the point at which the discarded data becomes visible. A quality setting of 80–85 on a JPEG is a reliable starting point for photographs, and going lower than 75 usually begins to show artefacts in text and edges. Always preview the result at 100% zoom, then zoom out again; if the image looks good at both sizes, you have probably found a safe setting.

For screenshots and graphics, reach for the lossless option. PNG and WebP in lossless mode will never degrade your image, and you can always apply a secondary lossy compression pass later for the web deliverable.

Serving the Right Size for Every Device

Mobile devices have small screens, so they do not need a wide image. The simplest approach is to serve a width that matches the image's rendered size, not the size of the original file. A photograph that displays at 600 pixels wide in a sidebar should be delivered at 600 pixels wide, not at 3000. You can also use the srcset attribute to serve smaller images on small screens, but for a first pass, simply delivering the right pixel dimensions is the most effective single change.

Lazy Loading and Beyond

Lazy loading tells the browser to defer the loading of images until they are about to appear in the viewport. This reduces the initial payload and improves the time to first contentful paint. Pair it with explicit width and height attributes to prevent layout shift, which is the annoying effect where the page jumps while the image loads.

Measuring Your Improvement

Measure before and after using your browser's built-in developer tools (Network tab) or a free tool such as PageSpeed Insights. Record the Total-byte weight and the Largest Contentful Paint. A realistic improvement from image optimisation alone is a reduction of one third to two thirds of the page weight, which usually translates into a materially faster first paint.

Summary

Website speed is not a backend-only problem. The images you choose, their format, their size, and how they are served all set the ceiling on how fast your pages can load. Start by choosing the right format, compress with a live preview to find the sweet spot, resize to the displayed dimensions, and lazy-load the rest.
