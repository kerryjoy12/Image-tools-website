How to Prepare Images for Digital Publishing

Magazines, e-books, and newsletters have different requirements than web publishing. This guide explains how to prepare images for digital publishing workflows, including the exact resolution, colour space, and compression settings that keep page file size down while preserving print-quality detail.

What Changes When You Publish Digital?

Digital publishing splits into two very different jobs. On one side is web publishing, where the image is compressed to the smallest file that still looks good on a screen and loads quickly. On the other is digital print, where the image must be rendered at a fixed, high density of ink, and the information content is much higher. The physical page is a fixed size, and the images on it must be sharp at a close viewing distance. A photograph that looks good on a website and is small enough to load quickly will almost always be too small and too grainy when printed.

The preparation workflow for digital publishing is therefore the opposite of the web workflow. Start with a high-resolution source, apply the correct colour space, then use compression to control the final file size without destroying the print quality.

Resolution: Pixels Per Inch in the Real World

The first question to answer is how large the image will be printed. A 2 × 2 inch square printed at 300 PPI requires 600 × 600 pixels. A full-page image 8 × 6 inches at 300 PPI requires 2400 × 1800 pixels. The difference is six times the pixels, which translates directly into a larger file after compression.

Most printing houses recommend 300 PPI as a standard, and anything below 200 PPI will begin to show visible grain when printed at full size. This is different from the web, where a 300 PPI image is an unnecessary burden. If a page of a book is 6 inches wide, an 1800-pixel wide image is a sensible source, not a luxury.

Colour Space: The Scope of the Palette

Colour space describes the range of colours an image can represent. The two spaces you will encounter most often are sRGB and Adobe RGB. sRGB is the standard for web and consumer devices, and it is the safe default for digital publishing. Adobe RGB describes a wider gamut, which is useful for professional printing, but it can display poorly on a website or a consumer screen because the device cannot render those colours.

For a digital publication that will be read on a screen, export in sRGB. If the publication requires a separate print run, prepare a second version in Adobe RGB for the print house, and keep the original file as the master. The PixelTools Image Compressor works best with sRGB images, because the colour values stay within the display capabilities of every reader.

Compression: How Much Is Enough?

The compressor is your partner in keeping the file size of a digital publication under control. A full-page photograph in a printed book, compressed as JPEG at quality 90, might be 8–12 MB. The same photograph at quality 82 might be 3–5 MB. The quality difference is imperceptible on a printed page, but the savings are significant. If a book contains 30 photographs, the difference between quality 90 and quality 82 can be 200 MB of storage and hundreds of megabytes of bandwidth.

Lossless formats have a different role. A screenshot of a page layout, or a vector-like graphic in the book, should be preserved as PNG or WebP, so that the text and lines remain sharp. The compression decision for photographs is almost always a quality slider, while the decision for graphics is a format decision.

A Practical Workflow for Digital Publishers

  1. Start with the source at print resolution. Do not downscale an image before you know the final print size. Work at full resolution and let the compressor reduce the weight.
  2. Export in sRGB. Keep the colour space consistent so that every reader sees the same image.
  3. Compress with a live preview. Use the quality slider and verify the result at 100% zoom. A good starting point is quality 82 for JPEG.
  4. Batch-process the whole book. Drag in a folder of photographs and compress them all in one sitting, saving a separate master where version control matters.
  5. Check the combined file size. A digital book that contains thousands of images can balloon to hundreds of megabytes. Measure the total weight, not just the weight of a single image.

FAQ

What is the right resolution for a digital magazine? 300 PPI is the industry standard. That means an image that prints at 6 inches wide should be 1800 pixels wide. Check the specific printing house requirements before you finalise the file.

Should I use JPEG or PNG in a published book? Photographs use JPEG with a high quality setting. Graphics, screenshots, and anything with text use PNG or WebP with lossless compression. Do not use JPEG to compress text.

Can I reduce the file size without hurting print quality? Yes. A quality setting between 80 and 85 is well below the threshold where print damage begins. The benefit is a smaller file and a faster download.

What about transparency in a PDF? Most publishing formats flatten transparency into a solid colour before export. If the image has a transparent background, the publishing software will fill it with white or the page background. Check the final PDF before you publish.

Summary

Preparing images for digital publishing means starting with enough resolution, choosing the right colour space, and using compression to control the total file size without destroying the detail. The workflow is the opposite of web publishing: more pixels, high-quality output, and careful attention to the print size.
